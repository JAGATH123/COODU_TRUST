Understanding

What microfinance & financial inclusion means

What is microfinance?

Microfinance provides financial services — small loans, savings opportunities, insurance and other financial products — to low-income individuals and communities who typically lack access to traditional banking, helping them start or expand small businesses and build financial resilience.

Coodu Trust is not itself a lender. What the annual reports record is financial inclusion work with women's Self-Help Groups (SHGs) — organising the groups, training them, and linking them to banks and government credit schemes so that the money reaches them. Under Mahalir Thittam, 50 groups in Kadavur and Thogamalai blocks were trained in financial inclusion and 67 groups of Karur district were linked to a nationalised bank for a ₹1.33 crore loan for rearing cows.

The trust has been at this since 2002–03, when its report described providing micro-finance to self-help groups to free poor families from money lenders. The most recent recorded work is with NABARD: in 2020–21 NABARD Karur sanctioned 150 joint liability groups to the trust in Kadavur block, of which 100 were formed and opened bank accounts for 600 members. The 2023–24 report still lists micro-credit among the trust's fields of work, but describes no micro-credit activity of its own for that year.

Our financial inclusion process

  1. Savings mobilization Regular savings habits built among women through SHG group savings schemes.
  2. Financial literacy Financial knowledge and skills for better money management and business planning.
  3. Bank and scheme linkage Groups linked to nationalised and rural banks, NABARD, SGSY and TAHDCO so that credit reaches them for income-generating activities.
  4. Enterprise support Credit, training and market linkages for women entrepreneurs to start and grow businesses.

Why this work matters

The challenge on the ground — and our answer

The challenge

Locked out of formal banking

Low-income women and their communities typically lack access to traditional banking and financial services altogether.

Our response

Credit through the group

Groups open savings accounts first, then borrow together. Every one of the 67 new Kadavur and Thogamalai groups had a bank account by 2007–08; 38 of them were linked to NABARD and the DRDA for economic assistance that year.

The challenge

The poorest stay trapped in poverty

Without access to credit, the most vulnerable women cannot invest in livelihoods or break the cycle of poverty.

Our response

Target the most vulnerable first

The Rashtriya Mahila Kosh lends solely to poor women. Its first-phase outlay of ₹7.5 lakh through Coodu Trust put a milch animal in the hands of 30 women farmers and credit for petty shops and sheep rearing in the hands of ten more.

The challenge

Money skills no one ever taught

Rural women are rarely given the financial knowledge needed for money management, savings and business planning.

Our response

Financial literacy alongside credit

Training builds better money management, regular savings habits and informed financial decision-making.

The challenge

No capital to start a business

Women with viable enterprise ideas lack the credit, training and market access needed to act on them.

Our response

Credit, training and market linkages

Credit is paired with enterprise skills — 201 women were trained in dairy, tailoring, nursery management, vermicomposting and mushroom production in 2009–10, and 120 commercial sheep farms took root in Kadavur block on Mahalir Thittam finance.

Impact

What financial inclusion changes

Financial access

67 self-help groups of Karur district were linked to a nationalised bank for a ₹1.33 crore loan to rear cows, and 100 NABARD joint liability groups opened accounts for 600 members in Kadavur block.

Entrepreneurship development

Credit is matched with skills: 201 women trained in dairy, tailoring, nursery management, vermicomposting and mushroom production in 2009–10, and 120 commercial sheep farms established in Kadavur block.

Financial literacy

Training on financial inclusion was given to 50 self-help groups of Kadavur and Thogamalai blocks under Mahalir Thittam — savings, bank accounts, revolving funds and the routes to grants and subsidies.

Economic independence

The Rashtriya Mahila Kosh outlay of ₹7.5 lakh put a milch animal in the hands of 30 women farmers and petty-shop and sheep-rearing credit in the hands of ten more.
