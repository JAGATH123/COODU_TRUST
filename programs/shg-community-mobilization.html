Understanding

What SHG & community mobilization means

What is a self-help group?

A self-help group (SHG) is a small group of 10–15 women who come together to save money, access credit and support each other in development activities — a grassroots platform for collective action.

Community mobilization takes the model further — organizing these groups and linking them with broader development initiatives and government schemes. Our programmes focus on forming, strengthening and sustaining women's collectives that serve as platforms for economic empowerment, social transformation and community development. The trust’s own annual reports trace the growth: 644 groups with 11,814 members across nine districts by 2005–06, 844 men’s and women’s groups in 11 districts in 2006–07, and 1,345 by 2012–13.

The watershed programmes of the early 2000s organised 10,925 people into self-help groups — the activity tables count participants, not groups. Through the SHG model, women gain access to financial services, develop leadership skills and become agents of change in their communities. Coodu Trust was the approved NGO for Mahalir Thittam in Karur district, and in 2020–21 NABARD Karur sanctioned 150 joint liability groups to the trust in Kadavur block, of which 100 were formed and opened bank accounts for 600 members.

Our community mobilization process

  1. SHG formation Organizing women into small, cohesive groups of 10–15 members for mutual support and collective action.
  2. Capacity building Training SHG members in group management, financial literacy and leadership skills.
  3. Savings & credit Facilitating group savings and providing access to credit for productive activities.
  4. Mobilization & empowerment Linking SHGs with community development programmes and government schemes, and promoting women's participation in decision-making and community governance.

Why this work matters

The challenge on the ground — and our answer

The challenge

Women facing challenges alone

On their own, poor rural women lack the mutual support and collective strength to address the common challenges they share.

Our response

Organize into self-help groups

Small, cohesive groups of 10–15 members create strong community networks — 644 groups with 11,814 members by 2005–06, growing to 1,345 men’s and women’s groups in 11 districts.

The challenge

No route to formal credit

Without savings or a bank account, women cannot fund productive activities — many have never used formal banking services at all.

Our response

Savings and bank linkage

Group savings build internal lending funds, and SHG–bank linkage opens formal credit. In 2007–08 all 67 new groups in Kadavur and Thogamalai opened savings bank accounts; in 2020–21, 100 NABARD joint liability groups opened accounts for 600 members.

The challenge

No seat at the decision-making table

Women are left out of community governance and the decisions that shape development in their own villages.

Our response

Grow women leaders

Group leaders are trained to run their own collectives — 2,200 self-help groups, panchayat level federations and community resource persons trained across five blocks of Virudhunagar in 2018–19, and 60 women SHG leaders in Manur block in 2023–24.

The challenge

Community issues left unaddressed

Problems in education, health and women's rights persist when there is no collective platform to act on them.

Our response

Collective action for social change

SHG networks mobilize communities for participatory development — addressing issues like education, health and women's rights, and implementing infrastructure and social development projects.

Impact

What SHG & community mobilization changes

Group formation

Formed and sustained 1,345 men’s and women’s self-help groups in 11 districts of Tamil Nadu, from 644 groups with 11,814 members in 2005–06.

Savings mobilization

Groups are formed to build a savings habit first. In 2008–09 the trust facilitated a ₹44,50,000 revolving fund for 85 groups — scheme money routed through the banks, not trust income.

Financial inclusion

Linked groups with nationalised and rural banks — every one of the 67 new Kadavur and Thogamalai groups opened a savings account in 2007–08, and 100 NABARD joint liability groups followed in 2020–21.

Women leadership

2,200 self-help groups, panchayat level federations and community resource persons were trained across five blocks of Virudhunagar in 2018–19, and 4,200 participants in three districts in 2020–21.
